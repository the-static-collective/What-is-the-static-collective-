# 🎬 THE PET SITTER — Featured Story Seed

> **He was only supposed to be there until the regular guy came back. The regular guy never came back.**

**Status:** Featured original story seed · concept / not screenplay · 2026-09-23\
**Working title:** THE PET SITTER\
**Working missing-person name:** Deuce Burbank (subject to change)\
**Genre:** Deadpan disaster comedy → creeping animal conspiracy → wildly sincere found-family rescue adventure.\
**Creative origin:** Conversation-developed premise: the initial comic spark invoked _Ace Ventura_, _Willard_, and _Guardians of the Galaxy_ as tonal reference points. This page deliberately treats them as inspiration, **not** shared universe, borrowed characters, or story canon. All characters, mythology, dialogue, and plot developed from this seed should stand independently.

## The logline

A recently divorced substitute teacher agrees to watch an eccentric animal detective's pets for the weekend. The detective never comes back. While trying to maintain order in an apartment that contains more animals than it should, the substitute discovers that the creatures have alliances, grudges, communications networks, and a rescue mission already in motion. They seem to have decided that the substitute is their new leader.

He wants his security deposit back.

## The disproportionately important casting decision

**The protagonist is a recently divorced substitute teacher. Not a cop, chosen one, animal whisperer, genius, or secret heir.**

The classroom is the engine of his approach. He tries seating charts, attendance, feeding schedules, name tags, assigned partners, quiet time, and detention. He gives a raccoon detention. He attempts a parent–teacher conference with a parrot.

None of this works the way he expects. Gradually he stops imposing human classroom order and starts noticing the animals' actual social world. His defining competence is not command: he can meet a chaotic room where it is, learn who is missing, notice who needs help, and keep showing up.

**The pivot:** first he thinks he is substituting for Deuce. Eventually he realizes the animals aren't waiting for Deuce; they have chosen him.

## The absent legend: Deuce Burbank

Deuce Burbank is the tentative name of the mysterious, absent animal detective and apparent keeper of the apartment. His offscreen reputation is ridiculous; his written instructions are suspiciously specific. We encounter him through notes, recorded messages, objects, animals' reactions, old acquaintances, and the consequences of what he did or failed to finish. His disappearance launches the story, but the protagonist owns the movie.

The substitute was told: _a couple of days._ Three days pass. The phone number reaches a disconnected payphone. By week's end, he has discovered at least one sealed room and an animal in the ventilation system that can imitate a familiar voice. The legal-looking person who arrives to collect some of the animals is a more urgent problem than Deuce's whereabouts.

**Open question:** did Deuce plan this handoff, vanish for unrelated reasons, or leave a task he did not mean to pass on? The seed must not prematurely decide.

## The apartment is the first world

The visible pets are only the first census. There are concealed spaces, improvised routes, residents whose existence the others are concealing, and an animal-made geography that differs from the floor plan. Each new discovery should revise the protagonist's understanding of who lives here, who depends on whom, and what “taking care of them” actually means.

The animals are not generic cute sidekicks:

* **Raccoon:** brilliant opportunist, steals anything shiny, initially indistinguishable from a menace; learns exactly which possessions the substitute values.
* **Parrot:** repeats recorded speech with perfect timing but without explaining context; a noisy, fallible archive of Deuce's past.
* **Rats:** real individual creatures whose routes and collective behavior turn the building into an intelligence network; their “plan” may initially look like an infestation.
* **Elderly tortoise:** moves at tortoise speed; appears to know something essential but cannot produce answers on the protagonist's schedule.
* **Dog:** the emotional anchor, with ordinary fears, loyalties, and routines that the substitute must learn rather than interpret as clues.

Names, numbers, species, and capabilities are provisional. The animals should remain recognizably animals; their achievements come from observation, habit, environment, opportunism, patient coordination, and the protagonist learning to work with their real abilities. Avoid a sudden fantasy upgrade that makes their earlier vulnerability meaningless.

## Four-act tonal migration

### 1. The weekend: substitute teacher versus zoo

The substitute receives absurd instructions, makes a disastrous attendance sheet, and tries to divide the apartment into classroom zones. Absence becomes a running gag until it ceases to be funny. Deuce is not coming back, and no one else has volunteered.

**Scene to keep:** the raccoon gets detention; while the substitute lectures it, the raccoon steals the detention slip and uses it to reach something in a locked cabinet.

### 2. The room has a curriculum

The animals unplug a phone when he calls for help. Apparently unrelated behaviors begin adding up to purposeful coordination. Their little alliances and feuds make it impossible to treat them as a single magical hive mind. The substitute starts keeping attendance not because he loves order but because he notices someone is missing.

**Comic principle:** his teacher habits become genuinely useful only once he adapts them to the actual individuals.

### 3. Willard turn: the whistle

A visitor arrives with plausible paperwork claiming ownership of several animals. The substitute could walk away. Instead, he whistles to get the room's attention. Everything goes silent. Then the walls begin to move with the sound of rats taking their positions.

This should be scary for a moment—not because the animals are monsters, but because the substitute realizes how much agency was operating around him while he thought he was in charge.

**The choice:** he refuses the handoff because these are no longer a pile of property to dispose of; they are individuals whose lives he has come to know.

### 4. The city becomes the classroom

The apartment opens onto a wider neighborhood network: pigeon lookouts, rat routes, a stubborn dog, a raccoon whose inability to resist shiny objects compromises an entire operation, and a parrot who repeats one useful fragment at the least useful possible moment. The substitute attempts a rescue with no special powers, no official standing, and the tactical mindset of a teacher organizing the world's least reliable field trip.

The adventure grows outrageous, but the stakes remain personal and concrete: bring _these particular animals_ back. He doesn't save an abstract “animal kingdom.” He notices who is missing and goes after them.

## Emotional engine

A man whose old household has broken apart is handed a new household that he did not ask for and cannot easily manage. The animals don't cure his grief or replace the people he has lost. They simply keep demanding that he show up. He discovers a form of belonging by repeatedly doing the unglamorous work of caring for them.

**Central reversal:** he begins as a disposable substitute. He becomes the irreplaceable person the animals decided to trust.

## Closing image

After an absurd and exhausting rescue, he returns to a wrecked apartment. He has lost his job, and he finally starts crying on the floor. The animals gather around him. The raccoon brings him a stolen wallet. The dog settles its head on his knee. The tortoise at last reaches the middle of the room.

The parrot, previously a relentless source of Deuce's obnoxious catchphrases, repeats a voice the substitute has never heard say these words: **“He's a good one. Take care of him.”**

He looks up: _“Wait. You knew I was going to be here?”_

The tortoise blinks.

**CUT TO BLACK.**

**Optional post-credits tag:** Deuce finally returns with groceries, finds the apartment empty, and reads a note: “Gone to save the world. Back in a couple days.” Do not use this gag to undermine the substitute's ownership of the story or definitively settle the mystery of Deuce's absence.

## Creative constraints worth protecting

1. **Keep the teacher.** His mundane professional skills, not secret greatness, must solve real problems and generate jokes.
2. **Keep Deuce mostly absent.** The missing figure is a shadow, a question, and a source of consequences—not the hero who returns to solve Act IV.
3. **Keep the tonal turn earned.** Comic animal behavior becomes unsettling and then moving without changing the animals into arbitrary superheroes.
4. **Keep the animal community particular.** Rivalries, vulnerabilities, ecology, accidents, and individual trust matter more than a flawless collective intelligence.
5. **Keep the heart unsentimental.** The substitute does not magically cease to be lonely; he becomes responsible, capable of receiving care, and very much needed.
6. **Make the IP original.** No Ace Ventura characters, dialogue, likenesses, trademarked fictional universe, or plot dependency. “Deuce Burbank” is a working name for a new character, not a renamed copy of Ace.

## First executable writing slice

Write an **8–12 page opening sequence**, ending with the substitute taking attendance in the apartment and discovering one extra name on his handwritten list—one he does not remember writing. The next writing target is the **whistle scene**, drafted separately to test whether the tonal transition can be played straight without losing the comedy.

**Story question to preserve:** What does an ordinary person become when everyone around him keeps treating him as the person who will come back?
