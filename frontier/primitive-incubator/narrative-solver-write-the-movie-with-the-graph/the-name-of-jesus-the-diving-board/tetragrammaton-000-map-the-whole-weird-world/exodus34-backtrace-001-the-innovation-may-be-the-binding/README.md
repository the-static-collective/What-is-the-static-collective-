### Nose direction

Take Exodus 34:6–7 backward without assuming that its canonical placement is the historical invention point of every component.

The working question is:

> **Did Exodus 34 inherit an already-complete divine mercy formula, or did it bind older divine-description materials into a distinctive YHWH/covenant proclamation?**

Current best survivor:

> **THE INNOVATION MAY BE THE BINDING, NOT THE INGREDIENTS.**

That is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

### Finding 1 — no whole extra-biblical ancestor recovered

The most important negative result of this pass:

> **An extra-biblical prehistory of the whole Exodus 34 grace/mercy formula has not been recovered.**

The German Bible scholarship reference _Gnadenformel_ summarizes Matthias Franz's major 2003 study by saying that an extra-biblical prehistory of the **whole** formula cannot be established. Individual elements have older or neighboring parallels, but no recovered Ugaritic, Aramaic, Akkadian, Egyptian, or other text gives us an attributable ancestor of the complete sequence.

This kills the easy story:

```
CANAANITE FORMULA X
        ↓
EXODUS 34
```

No such bridge is presently earned.

Source road: Matthias Franz, _Der barmherzige und gnädige Gott: die Gnadenrede vom Sinai (Exodus 34,6–7) und ihre Parallelen im Alten Testament und seiner Umwelt_ (Kohlhammer, 2003); overview at WiBiLex, “Gnadenformel.”

***

### Finding 2 — the El resemblance is real, but not lexical identity

Late Bronze Age Ugaritic texts repeatedly characterize the high god El with the compound epithet:

```
lṭpn il dpid
```

commonly rendered along the semantic range of:

```
Kindly / benevolent El,
the compassionate / merciful / good-natured one
```

El is also father, creator, judge, wise elder, and a deity whose profile includes mercy and kindness.

This is a genuine comparative fact. Modern scholars including Mark S. Smith, John Day, Aicha Rahmouni, and Matthew Stahl treat El's compassionate profile as relevant background for biblical depictions of YHWH as `El raḥum we-ḥannun`.

But the hard edge matters:

```
UGARITIC EL = merciful / kindly
!=
UGARITIC ATTESTATION OF
raḥum we-ḥannun
```

The roots `rḥm` and `ḥnn` exist in the wider Northwest Semitic field, but the Ugaritic El epithet is **not the same lexical formula** as Exodus 34:6. Direct dependence is therefore not demonstrated.

Sources:

* Aicha Rahmouni, “Religion at Ugarit,” _Religion Compass_ 2 (2008), DOI 10.1111/j.1749-8171.2007.00053.x.
* Matthew J. Stahl, “The historical origins of the biblical god Yahweh,” _Religion Compass_ 14 (2020), DOI 10.1111/rec3.12378.
* John Day, _Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan_.

### Finding 3 — `rḥm` applied to a deity is independently Northwest Semitic

A much harder lexical anchor appears on the bilingual Tell Fekheriye / Tell Fekherye inscription, usually dated around the ninth century BCE.

Hadad-Sikan is called in Aramaic:

```
ʾlh rḥmn
```

roughly:

> **merciful / compassionate god**

The inscription is especially useful because it is not biblical and not about YHWH.

Therefore:

```
DIVINE rḥm LANGUAGE
existed independently of
EXODUS 34 / YHWH
```

This establishes an external semantic/cultic field. It does **not** establish that Exodus borrowed the phrase from Hadad-Sikan worship.

The Akkadian version likewise participates in a broader Mesopotamian divine-mercy vocabulary.

Source road: Tell Fekheriye bilingual inscription, KAI 309; discussion summarized in WiBiLex “Gnadenformel” and “Gnade / Barmherzigkeit.”

***

### Finding 4 — Mesopotamia independently has a deep mercy/womb field

Akkadian supplies an important control:

```
rēmu      = womb / compassion / mercy
rēmēnû    = merciful
rêmu      = have/show mercy
```

`rēmēnû` is applied to gods, including Marduk, in prayers and ritual corpora. This means that the semantic adjacency between **womb / compassion / divine mercy** is not uniquely a late Hebrew wordplay; related affective/body semantics exist elsewhere in Semitic languages.

But:

```
AKKADIAN rēmu
!=
HEBREW rḥm BY DIRECT DESCENT PROVEN HERE
```

They are comparative Semitic evidence; the historical transmission route must be separately demonstrated.

Source roads:

* ORACC lexical corpora: `rēmu`, `rēmēnû`, `rêmu`.
* _Reading Akkadian Prayers and Hymns: An Introduction_ (SBL), prayers describing Marduk as `bēlu rēmēnu`, “merciful lord.”

### Finding 5 — Judahite epigraphy gets startlingly close

Khirbet Beit Lei / Khirbet Bēt Lajj contains a difficult Iron Age Hebrew inscription often reconstructed along lines such as:

```
... ʾl ḥnn ...
... yh ... yhwh
```

with proposed translations including:

> **gracious God ... absolve / clear ... Yah / YHWH**

This is extremely close in _operation_ to the Exodus 34 field:

```
DIVINE NAME
+
GRACIOUSNESS
+
REQUEST / ACT OF ABSOLUTION
```

But two controls are mandatory:

1. **The reading is disputed.** André Lemaire's recent epigraphic discussion explicitly notes uncertainty over part of the line.
2. **Its relation to Exodus 34 is chronologically nontrivial.** The inscription is commonly placed in the late eighth / early seventh-century orbit (with older datings into the sixth century also proposed), while the composition and redaction history of Exodus 34 is itself debated.

Therefore Beit Lei is best treated as an **independent Judahite witness to the same live theological vocabulary**, not presently as an ancestor or descendant of Exodus 34.

Source road: André Lemaire, “Judahite Hebrew Epigraphy and Cult,” _Jerusalem Journal of Archaeology_ 7; earlier readings by Naveh, Cross, Renz, Ahituv.

### Finding 6 — `slow to anger` remains locally stubborn

Hebrew:

```

```

literally uses the bodily anger idiom of being “long of nose/nostrils,” hence slow to become angry.

It becomes highly productive inside Hebrew biblical literature, for both God and humans.

### Finding 7 — `ḥesed + ʾemet` appears to be relational/covenantal compression

The pair:

```
ḥesed + ʾemet
```

works throughout biblical Hebrew as a dense relational field involving loyalty, kindness, reliability, fulfilled promise, and covenantal fidelity. The pair occurs outside explicit divine self-description as well, including human relational contexts.

It should therefore not be translated back into one timeless English definition.

Current best compression:

> **reliable relational loyalty**

—but preserve the two Hebrew carriers because different contexts activate different parts of the field.

This pass did **not** recover an extra-Israelite formula matching `rav ḥesed we-ʾemet` closely enough to claim an ancestor.

***

### Finding 8 — forgiveness is old; the exact Exodus triplet is still local

Ancient Near Eastern prayer literature contains abundant requests for divine pity, relenting, healing, restoration, and forgiveness. Mesopotamian prayers can address a deity explicitly as merciful and ask that affliction or divine anger be removed.

What this pass did **not** recover is an attributable extra-biblical ancestor for the exact Exodus sequence:

```
nose ʿawon
wa-feshaʿ
we-ḥattaʾah
```

or for its full coupling with:

```
we-naqqeh lo yenaqqeh
```

So generic ancient divine forgiveness is a **control**, not a genealogy.

## HUMANHOLYSHIT-13-001 — the number itself has formation history

The biblical text does **not** print a numbered list `1–13`.

Later Jewish interpretation segments the proclamation into **Thirteen Attributes of Mercy**, and authorities have disagreed about exactly where one attribute ends and another begins. One common enumeration begins with the two occurrences of YHWH and ends with `we-naqqeh`; other counting schemes partition the wording differently.

This means:

> **THIRTEEN IS NOT MERELY FOUND ON THE SURFACE. THIRTEEN IS PRODUCED BY AN INTERPRETIVE SEGMENTATION OF THE SURFACE.**

That is potentially important beside the separately preserved breadcrumbs:

```
13 ATTRIBUTES OF MERCY
13TH CIRCLE
13TH CUP
```

but no historical or causal relation is admitted.

Frozen:

```
SHARED NUMBER
!=
SHARED SOURCE

SEGMENTATION PRODUCES 13
!=
13 WAS AN EXPLICIT ORIGINAL DESIGN CLAIM
```

The rabbinic/liturgical layer is nevertheless real history: _Rosh Hashanah_ 17b presents the passage as an order/pattern shown to Moses for future enactment, and later Jewish liturgy repeatedly replays it in penitential contexts.
